First, let’s review what fuel trims are and how the fuel system monitor works. The fuel system monitor uses fuel trim tables stored in the powertrain control module (PCM) to make necessary changes to compensate for the inevitable wear and aging of the fuel system components.
Fuel trim tables are based on engine load, which is mostly determined by air mass. During closed-loop fuel control, the fuel trim strategy makes and stores corrections to 
 fuel trims to correct a rich or lean condition. These corrections are called long-term fuel trims (LTFT). Short-term fuel trim (STFT) refers to the desired air/fuel ratio parameter. However, STFT is not a stored value; it changes as conditions change, in real time. 
The PCM uses input from the heated oxygen sensors (HO2S) to help maintain a stoichiometric air/fuel ratio (14.7:1) during closed-loop operation. STFT and LTFT work together to achieve this. If the HO2S indicates the engine is running lean, the PCM corrects the lean condition by moving the STFT into the positive range, adding more fuel to correct for the lean condition. If the STFT is compensating for a considerable amount of time, the PCM learns this and moves the LTFT into the positive range to compensate and allow the STFT to return to a revised starting point near 0%.
The fuel system monitor also relies on inputs from the engine coolant temperature (ECT) or cylinder head temperature (CHT) sensor, the intake air temperature (IAT) sensor and the mass airflow (MAF) sensor in order to activate the fuel trim system, which in turn activates the fuel system monitor. If the fuel system monitor sees the fuel trim tables reach the adaptive limit, and the desired air/fuel ratio parameter has exceeded the calibrated limit, it will set a P0171 or P0174 and activate the MIL.
Typically, a lean code will be set and the MIL will be activated when the total fuel trim exceeds +20% for two consecutive drive cycles. The tot-
 al fuel trim is determined by adding STFT and LTFT from the same bank. So if LTFT is +15% and STFT is +6%, the total fuel trim is +21%.
Okay, so you have lean codes. Don’t clear the DTCs yet. Using freeze frame data, record the engine temperature at which the DTC was set, whether it’s ECT or CHT. This is important because some components, such as intake manifold gaskets, might become an issue only at certain temperature ranges. You need to know the engine temperature when the DTC was set.
View your fuel trims and verify that you’re seeing a total fuel trim of +20% or higher at the bank that set the DTC. Be aware that some four-cylinder and straight six engines will split the cylinders into two banks (BK1 and BK2). Now that you’ve verified that the DTCs are P0171 and/or P0174, and the fuel trims exceed the threshold, it’s time for a visual inspection.
Never underestimate the powers of the visual inspection. When you’re performing any diagnostics, whether it’s a MIL or a squeaky front suspension, the visual inspection is your most valuable tool. Not only does it give you the opportunity to “see” the problem, it also allows you to get a “feel” for the entire vehicle. You want to see the big picture. It’s easy to get tunnel vision and see only the specific system or subsystem you’re diagnosing. Always familiarize yourself with the entire vehicle. Look for the obvious, such as cracked vacuum hoses, a loose air intake hose clamp, damaged or improperly installed parts, a leaking PCV valve or an exhaust leak before the O2 sensor.
If nothing appears cracked, beaten, abused or misused, the next step is to determine if the lean condition is caused by a vacuum leak. You can’t trust MAF or engine load (LOAD) parameters to indicate if there’s a vacuum leak. The MAF value often reflects its reaction to the concern, without actually being the cause of the problem. Here’s how to determine if the high fuel trims are caused by a vacuum leak (refer to the screen captures above):
•Record the total fuel trims for both banks individually at idle, with the engine at the temperature at which the DTC was set, as per freeze frame data.
•Depress and hold the throttle pedal to maintain an engine rpm of 2500.
•Observe the PIDs with the engine rpm at 2500. Record total fuel trims. Keep the hood open to keep the engine temperature from going too far above your predetermined diagnostic temp for as long as possible. If it does, let the engine cool down. Getting as close as possible to freeze frame temp is very important.
•If the fuel correction decreases at least 15% at 2500 rpm, you have a vacuum leak. So if your total fuel trim on BK1 at idle is +24%, and at 2500 rpm it drops to about +20% or lower, then a vacuum leak is the cause of the high fuel trims.
This is because the unmetered air entering the engine at 2500 rpm is a much smaller percentage of total air intake than the amount of unmetered air that’s entering at idle; therefore, it has a lesser effect on fuel trims.
If you’ve determined that there’s a vacuum leak, but saw nothing during your visual inspection, the next step is to find the leak. Isolate components by blocking vacuum hoses, either by removing and plugging them, or by crimping them off. Block vacuum to one component at a time, while watching STFT. Wait about 15 seconds for fuel trims to fully react before removing your block and moving on to the next location. You should see a significant drop in STFT when you block off the right component. Isolate anything that has a vacuum hose. This includes the brake power booster, evap canister purge, HVAC controls, vacuum reservoir, etc. You can feel confident that if you block vacuum to a component and STFT doesn’t change, that component is not the cause of the lean condition.
It’s important to note that unmetered air can also enter the intake through the crankcase via the PCV system. The air that the PCV system pulls through the crankcase is not metered by the MAF sensor, but is figured into the sum of the total air intake. If there’s a leak that allows air to be sucked into the crankcase through any opening other than the predetermined crankcase breather hose, it will cause a lean condition.
I recently worked on a vehicle that had a small hole in the back of the right-side valve cover caused by a steel vacuum line that rubbed through. The air entering this hole caused a lean condition. A couple of common culprits for this are the seals in the valve cover that seal around the variable cam timing solenoids, and the dipstick seal. They tend not to leak oil but will suck air.
The two best ways to find a vacuum leak are propane enrichment and smoke, and I always like to try smoke first. Pumping smoke through the intake and crankcase with a good smoke machine allows you to see exactly where the smoke is coming out, which is exactly where the air is getting in. It’s important to smoke the intake at the engine temperature at which the code was set.
We recently serviced a Mercury Mountaineer with a 4.6L three-valve engine and a vacuum leak. We replaced the intake manifold gaskets because the tech discovered a vacuum leak at the right rear corner of the intake using carb cleaner and watching the O2 sensors. After the repair and after clearing keep-alive memory (KAM), the fuel trims shot right back up. When we smoked the intake we found that the smoke was coming from the intake manifold runner control (IMRC) shaft that ran through the intake. The shaft sat only about ½ in. above the intake gasket, making it an easy mistake to make with carb cleaner or even propane, but not with smoke. When using smoke, be sure to smoke both the intake and the crankcase, and use a bright light because smoke is not always easy to see. Use a mirror and look behind, around and through everything. Sometimes auto repair is done with smoke and mirrors.
Propane enrichment also works well and usually takes less time. It will always get you in the ballpark, but not always to the right seat. Be very careful when using propane; it’s very flammable. One rogue spark from an ignition wire or coil could surely ruin your day. Be careful about holding the propane under the intake manifold for too long, as this could cause a buildup of gas. If you do hold it under the intake—a good place to check for leaks—give the area a good shot with shop air to blow out any residual propane when you’re finished.
When using propane enrichment, slowly move the propane source along all likely vacuum leak locations while monitoring the O2 sensors. I prefer monitoring the sensors over STFT because the they’ll jump to rich immediately in response to the propane. It’s best to get a hose adapter for your propane tank. This allows you to hold the tank away from the engine and pinpoint the placement of the propane with the end of the hose. Hit all the obvious spots, such as intake gaskets, throttle body gaskets, fuel injectors, crankcase gaskets, etc. You’ll find that you’ll get a reading around most EGR valves; they tend to suck in a little air around the diaphragm, which is normal.
Avoid using carb cleaner. It does work, and will spike those O2 sensors rich when it gets sucked in, but it’s sloppy. That mist goes everywhere. You never really know exactly where it’s entering the intake. It also greatly increases the risk of fire.
What if you don’t have a vacuum leak? You performed the 2500-rpm test and your total fuel correction did not drop 15%. Let’s look at some possible causes of the lean codes. An underreporting MAF sensor will cause a lean condition. This means that the MAF sensor is reporting less air mass passing the throttle plates than is actually present. Less air means less fuel; less fuel means lean.
MAF sensors are tricky to monitor because their job is to react. An incorrect MAF sensor value could be due to its reacting to a vacuum leak, a miss or pretty much any driveability issue. So it’s best to ignore MAF sensor values until you’re sure there are no other issues. MAF reference values are also tricky because they vary so much depending on the application. Voltage specifications at idle can vary from .5V to .9V, and at 30 mph from .99V to 1.5V. So it’s important to look up reference values for the engine you’re working on.
Most MAF issues are caused by contamination. Debris covering the hot wire or the constant cold wire will cause it to malfunction. I like to record MAF sensor values at idle, clean the sensor with a MAF sensor cleaner, then recheck the values. If there’s a considerable change in the value, I know I have a contaminated MAF sensor. I recently worked on a 2002 Crown Victoria that would start but wouldn’t stay running due to a contaminated MAF sensor. There were no DTCs. I cleaned the sensor and the engine ran fine.
When you’re monitoring the MAF sensor, using the correct specs for the engine, keep in mind that even a small amount outside the specified range will cause an issue. Also, be sure that the MAF-V value moves almost exactly with the TPS-V value when accelerating. Also monitor the LOAD value, which is measured in percentage. This value also varies depending on engine type. Out-of-range LOAD values are often MAF sensor-related. Run a fuel injector flow test to determine that you don’t have a spitting injector, and keep in mind that if a cylinder is not firing efficiently, there’s more unburned air than fuel going past that O2 sensor.
Poor fuel quality can also cause a lean condition that could have you ready to throw in the towel. It’s always wise to collect some fuel from the vehicle, put it in a jar and inspect it. Check for separation caused by water or other contaminants and check for a weak odor. Determine whether the condition started shortly after the customer filled the tank.
Nowadays, the biggest issue with fuel quality is the addition of ethanol. Flex Fuel (FF) vehicles are made to run on as much as 85% ethanol (E85). But this feature can go bad if the PCM doesn’t know it’s burning E85. Around the 2004 model year, many manufacturers stopped using a flex-fuel sensor and the FF% is now inferred. The PCM calculates ethanol content after fuel is added, but only if the amount added exceeds a certain percentage of total fuel level input (FLI)—usually about 20%.
The PCM uses the O2 sensor values, in closed-loop operation, only after it has determined that all the fuel is out of the lines, and the new fuel is at the tip of the injector. It then monitors STFT and stores the FF inferred (FF_INF) value. If the engine is burning more ethanol than the FF_INF value suggests, it will set lean codes. To determine if this is the case, first verify that the FF learned (FF_LRND) parameter reads YES. If it reads NO, the vehicle must be driven until the FF percentage can be learned. The FF_INF value shows us what the PCM has determined the FF percentage is; we must now check the fuel using the following ethanol water test:
•Fill a 2-liter soda bottle less than halfway with water and mark the water level on the outside of the bottle.
•Add an equal amount of fuel to the bottle, cover the top and shake well to mix the fuel and water
•Let it stand for about two minutes. The ethanol in the fuel will absorb the water. The more ethanol, the higher the water level will rise past your mark. A slight rise in water level, about ¼ to ½ in., is about 10% to 15% ethanol. A rise in the water level of about 1½ in. or more indicates that the ethanol is in the 85% range. This is a good test, and it can be trusted.
•If the water test and FF_INF do not agree, clear keep-alive memory and road-test until the FF_LRND value registers YES, and the FF_INF value is correct. Keep in mind that non-FF vehicles will not tolerate any ethanol over 10%, maybe 15%.
I hope this article helps you to diagnose these nasty lean codes. Finally, remember to clear the KAM after all driveability repairs are completed.
